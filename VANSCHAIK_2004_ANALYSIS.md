# Van Schaik 2004 — "The Early Days of the Great Perfection"
## Structured Analysis for Cross-Tradition Comparative Project

**Source:** Sam van Schaik, "The Early Days of the Great Perfection," *Journal of the International Association of Buddhist Studies* 27.1 (2004): 165–206.

---

## I. Section-by-Section Summary of Argument

### §1. Origins of the Great Perfection (pp. 165–167)
- The earliest Great Perfection texts are the "mind series" (*sems sde*): short texts emphasizing the immediate presence of enlightened mind and the uselessness of any practice aimed at creating/cultivating enlightenment.
- Two Dunhuang texts (ITJ 594, ITJ 647) confirmed by Karmay as looking like mind series literature.
- Both Karmay and Germano suggested that the Great Perfection developed through intermingling of two traditions: (1) pristine, ritual-free discourse of siddha-style yogins, and (2) elaboration of rdzogs chen as culmination of Mahāyoga ritual practice.
- **Van Schaik's counter-thesis:** These two kinds of literature do not entail two separate traditions — they were produced by the *same people* within Māyājāla commentarial traditions.

### §2. What is the Great Perfection? — Evolution of the term *rdzogs chen*

#### (i) *rdzogs chen* as a ritual moment (pp. 167–169)
- Earliest appearance: Guhyagarbha tantra, where *rdzogs chen* refers to the state at the climax of perfection-stage sexual yoga immediately following consecration with bodhicitta.
- Semantic content: "great (chen) culmination of the perfection (rdzogs) stage."
- Key ideas already present: all qualities spontaneously present (*lhun gyis grub*), transcendence of concepts (*bsam gyis mi khyab*).
- Dunhuang evidence: PT 321 (Heruka sādhana) uses "maṇḍala of the secret great perfection"; ITJ 437 refers to bodhicitta substance as "the great perfection, the great self."
- PT 322B: untitled poem taking *rdzogs chen* as its theme within the Guhyagarbha/Māyājāla framework.

#### (ii) *rdzogs chen* as a framework for all tantric practice (pp. 170–175)
- Padmasambhava's *Man ngag lta ba'i phreng ba*: articulates *rdzogs chen* as culmination of three "ways" (*tshul*) — development, perfection, and great perfection. **Not a vehicle, but a mode.**
- gNyan dPal dbyangs' *rDo rje sems dpa' zhus lan* (early 9th c.): applies Great Perfection framework to all Mahāyoga practice. Key quote: "This is the way of the unsurpassable, self-arisen great perfection (*rdzogs chen*)" — a "way" (*tshul*) suggesting a *mode of practice*, not a separate set of practices.
- ITJ 454: untitled treatise applying Great Perfection-style discourse to deity yoga broadly, citing seven Mahāyoga tantras. Opens with: "At this very moment, without accomplishing it through a path or fabricating it with antidotes, one should remain in the spontaneous presence of the body, speech, and mind of primordial buddhahood."

#### (iii) *rdzogs chen* free from yogic practice (pp. 175–176)
- Mind series texts contain no references to development/perfection stages or merely negative references.
- ITJ 647 (commentary on *Rig pa'i khu byug*): still uses *rdzogs chen* as a "way" (*tshul*), but asserts independence from development and perfection stages.
- However: scribbled notes on the manuscript include the hundred-syllable mantra and "strive, day and night" — suggesting actual practice behind the rhetoric of non-practice.
- ITJ 331/1: brief sādhana attributed to Mañjuśrīmitra involving visualization of syllable *hūṃ* — resembles later Seminal Heart meditation.

#### (iv) *rdzogs chen* as a textual category (pp. 176–178)
- PT 353 / ITJ 507: refers to "the sūtras of the Great Perfection" — first evidence of *rdzogs chen* as a category of scripture.
- The text itself is a maṇḍala visualization featuring Ekajāṭī.

#### (v) *rdzogs chen* as a vehicle (pp. 178–179)
- **Earliest evidence of vehicle status: gNubs chen's *bSam gtan mig sgron* (STMG), probably early 10th century.**
- gNubs chen writes of "the vehicle of the activity-free Great Perfection" (*rdzogs pa chen po bya ba dang bral ba'i theg pa*).
- In STMG, *rdzogs chen* has become synonymous with Atiyoga and is no longer applicable within Mahāyoga context.

### §3. Development of Atiyoga — Parallel trajectory

#### (i) Atiyoga as a stage in practicing yoga (pp. 179–182)
- Earliest: Sarvabuddhasāmayoga tantra (8th c.) — Atiyoga compared with Anuyoga as stages of ritual practice.
- Kṛṣṇayamāri tantra: fourfold set (Yoga, Anuyoga, Atiyoga, Mahāyoga) as stages.
- Vilāsavajra's *sPar khab*: Atiyoga as "highest of all approaches to tantric practice."

#### (ii) Atiyoga as a distinct approach (pp. 182–186)
- PT 849: nine vehicles listed but Atiyoga only a subdivision of Yoga vehicle.
- ITJ 644: the nine discussed in terms of deity lineages — Atiyoga present but not as a vehicle.
- **PT 656 (*Spyi'i lung chen po bdun*): Key text.** Sets out seven approaches (= nine vehicles minus two). Atiyoga has its own view, meditation, activities, and pledges. Defined in terms of radical non-conceptualization, nonduality, and non-activity. **Still predicated on Mahāyoga terminology** but functionally self-sufficient.
- PT 44 (dated ca. 978–1010): Padmasambhava teaches "the secret bodhicitta that is included within Atiyoga" alongside Vajrakīlaya Mahāyoga practice. Shows late 10th c. compatibility/interdependence between Mahāyoga practice and Atiyoga meditation on mind's nature.

#### (iii) Atiyoga as a textual category (pp. 186–188)
- ITJ 594 (*sBas pa'i rgum chung*): identifies itself as belonging to the category (*phyogs*) of Atiyoga. Attributed to Buddhagupta.
- PT 699: commentary on a Chan text ascribed to Bodhidharma, ending with discussion of masters of sūtras, tantras, and Atiyoga. **Important Chan-Atiyoga intersection.**

#### (iv) Atiyoga as a vehicle (pp. 187–190)
- gNubs chen's STMG: first dateable use of Atiyoga as vehicle (*theg pa*). Atiyoga and *rdzogs chen* now synonymous.
- Sa skya Paṇḍita (13th c.) still disputed Atiyoga's vehicle status, arguing it was "wisdom and not a vehicle."

### §4. gNyan dPal dbyangs, gNubs chen, and the Great Perfection scriptures (pp. 190–199)

#### (i) dPal dbyangs and the six lamps
- dPal dbyangs (early 9th c.) wrote both: (a) treatises on Mahāyoga with Great Perfection emphasis, and (b) short texts in mind series style. Same author, two modes.
- His six lamps (*sgron ma drug*) include *Thugs kyi sgron ma* (Mahāyoga treatise) and five short Great Perfection-style discourses.
- **None of these texts reference Great Perfection scriptures or a vehicle of Atiyoga** — suggesting they predate such categories.
- His teacher was likely Sūryaprabhasasiṃha, author of the long Guhyagarbha commentary.

#### (ii) gNubs chen and STMG
- gNubs chen gathered disparate texts under the label "Atiyoga" and granted them vehicle status.
- He may have been involved in transforming authored "statements" (*lung*) into anonymous scripture.
- The texts cited in STMG's Atiyoga chapter do not explicitly distinguish Atiyoga from Mahāyoga — passages doing so (like those in *Kun byed rgyal po*) probably appeared *after* STMG.
- Twelve teachers listed in interlinear notes correspond to the mind series lineage in the Bairo rgyud 'bum.

### §5. Conclusions (pp. 200–203)
- The earliest Great Perfection texts were written by tantric commentators within Māyājāla traditions — the same people who wrote conventional Mahāyoga exegesis.
- No need to posit two separate streams; rather, two types of composition within one tradition.
- gNubs chen was central in coalescence of Great Perfection as a distinct tradition with vehicle status.
- "Pristine" Great Perfection never dominated — it always existed alongside forms engaging fully with structured practice.

## II. All Quotes Relevant to Chan-Dzogchen Comparison

### Direct Chan References

**This paper contains very little direct discussion of Chan.** The topic is peripheral to van Schaik's argument. Key mentions:

1. **PT 322B scribe and Chan** (p. 169, fn. 11):
   > "A group of four manuscripts in the same hand are a syncretic explication of Chan and Mahāyoga meditation practices (see van Schaik and Dalton 2004). Thus the scribe of PT 322B was certainly making use of Chan texts as well, although this is not apparent in PT 322B itself."

2. **PT 699 — Chan text with Atiyoga commentary** (p. 187, fn. 60):
   > "Another case of the use of the term Atiyoga appears in PT 699, a commentary on a Chan text ascribed to Bodhidharma. The commentary ends with a discussion of the characteristics of a master of the sūtras, a master of the tantras, and a master of Atiyoga. A translation of this passage, and a study of the commentary, which brings a Mahāyoga structure to the Chan text, appear in van Schaik and Dalton 2004."
   
   The root text exists in ITJ 689/1 and ITJ 1774.

3. **Meinert 2002 in references:**
   > Meinert, C. 2002. "Chinese Chan and Tibetan rDzogs chen: Preliminary Remarks on Two Tibetan Dunhuang Manuscripts," in *Religion and Culture in Secular Tibet* (ed. Henk Blezer). Brill, Leiden.

4. **Van Schaik & Dalton 2004 in references:**
   > van Schaik, Sam & Jacob Dalton. 2004. "Where Chan and Tantra Meet: Buddhist Syncretism in Dunhuang," in *The Silk Road: Trade, Travel, War and Faith*. London: British Library Press.

### No mention of Moheyan or the Samyé debate anywhere in the paper.

### Passages with Structural Parallels to Chan Discourse (not labeled as Chan by van Schaik)

These passages describe Great Perfection positions that closely parallel Chan/禅:

1. **ITJ 454 opening** (p. 174):
   > "At this very moment, without accomplishing it through a path or fabricating it with antidotes, one should remain in the spontaneous presence of the body, speech, and mind of primordial buddhahood."

2. **dPal dbyangs, *Zhus lan*** (p. 172):
   > "This mind itself which is without basis or root / Is, like the sky, not purified by cleansing. / Because enlightenment is free from production, / Enlightenment does not come from cause and effect."

3. **PT 656, Atiyoga section** (p. 184):
   > "In reality, it does not matter whether one practises or not."

4. **ITJ 647 on development/perfection stages** (p. 175):
   > "Because of the spontaneous accomplishment of ineffable bodhicitta, the maṇḍala is established spontaneously and abides without artifice, with no need for the activities of development and perfection."

## IV. Key Findings for Cross-Tradition Comparative Project

### 1. Chan and Dzogchen Were Geographically and Textually Proximate
The scribe of PT 322B (a *rdzogs chen* poem) was the same person who copied Chan-Mahāyoga syncretic texts. PT 699 is a Mahāyoga-structured commentary on a Chan text ascribed to Bodhidharma that explicitly discusses Atiyoga. **These are not separate traditions but overlapping scribal/intellectual communities at Dunhuang.**

### 2. The "Non-Practice" Rhetoric Is Strikingly Parallel
The Great Perfection discourse — "it does not matter whether one practises or not," "without accomplishing it through a path," "enlightenment does not come from cause and effect" — is structurally identical to Chan sudden-awakening rhetoric. Van Schaik does not draw this comparison, but the parallel is unmistakable.

### 3. "Pristine" Non-Practice Was Always Accompanied by Actual Practice
Van Schaik's key finding: the "pristine" Great Perfection never existed alone. Even ITJ 647, which declares development and perfection stages unnecessary, has notes requiring hundred-syllable mantra recitation. This parallels the Chan situation where "sudden awakening" was always accompanied by actual practice regimens.

### 4. The Mode-to-Vehicle Transition Is the Central Finding
Van Schaik traces how *rdzogs chen* evolved from:
- A **ritual moment** (climax of sexual yoga) →
- A **framework** for interpreting all tantric practice →
- An **independent approach** (still referencing tantra) →
- A **textual category** →
- A **vehicle** (*theg pa*)

This same dynamic may apply to Chan's evolution from a meditation mode within broader Buddhist practice to a self-identified school.

### 5. Specific Meditation Techniques
The paper mentions very few concrete techniques:
- **Visualization of syllable hūṃ** (ITJ 331/1, Mañjuśrīmitra) — resembles later snying thig
- **Hundred-syllable mantra recitation** (ITJ 647 notes)
- **Sexual yoga / perfection stage** as the original context for *rdzogs chen*
- No sky-gazing, light practices, or "watching mind" (*看心*) are discussed

### 6. PT 699 Is a Critical Document for Our Project
A commentary that applies **Mahāyoga structure to a Chan text** and ends by discussing masters of Atiyoga is exactly the kind of cross-tradition document our project needs. Van Schaik & Dalton 2004 "Where Chan and Tantra Meet" is the essential follow-up source.

## V. Assessment: Implications for the Three Isomorphisms Thesis

### Reminder: The Three Isomorphisms
1. **Structural isomorphism:** Chan and Dzogchen share the same logical structure (immediate presence, non-fabrication, beyond cause-and-effect)
2. **Practical isomorphism:** Similar meditation techniques (non-conceptual awareness, watching mind / rig pa)
3. **Historical isomorphism:** Actual historical contact and cross-pollination at Dunhuang

### What van Schaik 2004 STRENGTHENS:

1. **Historical isomorphism is strongly supported.** The same scribes copied Chan texts and Dzogchen texts. PT 699 explicitly bridges Chan and Atiyoga. The Dunhuang evidence shows these were not separate communities.

2. **Structural isomorphism is supported.** The Great Perfection's core claims — primordial enlightenment, spontaneous presence, uselessness of fabricated practice — are formally identical to Chan sudden-awakening doctrine. Van Schaik provides extensive textual evidence for these claims in pre-11th c. sources.

3. **The "mode to vehicle" argument explains why the traditions *look* separate even though they weren't.** The institutional hardening of Atiyoga into a vehicle created the *appearance* of a distinct tradition where previously there was a shared milieu. This is powerful support for the isomorphism thesis: the separation is late and institutional, not doctrinal.

### What van Schaik 2004 COMPLICATES:

1. **The Mahāyoga matrix is crucial.** Van Schaik shows that early Dzogchen was embedded in sexual yoga, deity visualization, and tantric ritual — contexts entirely absent from Chan. The structural isomorphism holds at the level of *view* but breaks down at the level of *practice context*. Early *rdzogs chen* was the realization at the climax of sexual union, not a standalone meditation like Chan sitting practice.

2. **"Pristine" Dzogchen may be late.** If the ritual-free, purely non-conceptual form of Dzogchen is a *later development* (post-STMG, early 10th c. onward), then it is precisely the form most similar to Chan that is most likely to have been *influenced by* Chan rather than independently arising. This complicates any claim of independent structural isomorphism.

3. **Van Schaik deliberately avoids the Chan comparison.** This is a paper about Dzogchen's tantric origins, not about Chan influence. The silence on Moheyan, the Samyé debate, and Chan meditation is conspicuous. This may reflect van Schaik's judgment (in 2004) that the Chan connection has been overemphasized relative to the Mahāyoga matrix. His later work (van Schaik & Dalton 2004) addresses the Chan connection directly.

4. **No meditation technique isomorphism is demonstrated.** The paper provides almost no evidence of shared *techniques* between Chan and Dzogchen. The practices it documents (sexual yoga, maṇḍala visualization, hūṃ visualization, mantra recitation) are all distinctly tantric.

### Bottom Line Assessment

Van Schaik 2004 is **essential for understanding the Mahāyoga matrix** from which Dzogchen emerged, but it is **not a source for Chan-Dzogchen comparison** per se. It provides:
- Strong evidence for **historical proximity** (shared scribal communities)
- Strong evidence for **structural similarity at the level of view**
- Strong evidence that the **practice contexts were radically different**
- A critical pointer to **PT 699** and **van Schaik & Dalton 2004** as the direct sources for Chan-Dzogchen intersection

**Priority follow-up sources:**
1. Van Schaik & Dalton 2004, "Where Chan and Tantra Meet" — **highest priority**
2. Meinert 2002, "Chinese Chan and Tibetan rDzogs chen"
3. PT 699 (commentary on Chan text with Atiyoga discussion)
4. PT 322B + its sister manuscripts (Chan-Mahāyoga syncretic group)
5. gNubs chen's STMG Chan chapter (not discussed in this paper but STMG has a separate chapter on Chan)
