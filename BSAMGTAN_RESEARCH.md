# bSam gtan mig sgron (བསམ་གཏན་མིག་སྒྲོན) — Research Report

## 1. The Text: Basic Facts

- **Tibetan title**: སྒོམ་གྱི་གནད་གསལ་བར་ཕྱེ་བ་བསམ་གཏན་མིག་སྒྲོན (*Sgom gyi gnad gsal bar phye ba bsam gtan mig sgron*)
- **English**: "The Lamp for the Eye of Contemplation: Elucidating the Key Points of Meditation"
- **Author**: Nub Sangyé Yeshé (གནུབས་སངས་རྒྱས་ཡེ་ཤེས, Gnubs Sangs rgyas ye shes), c. 844–943 CE
- **Date of composition**: Early 10th century (post-fall of Tibetan Empire)
- **Significance**: The single most important surviving Tibetan doxographical treatise on meditation from the "dark age" period. Classifies and compares four major contemplative systems.

## 2. Text Structure — The Four Meditation Systems

The *bSam gtan mig sgron* is organized around a **four-fold classification of contemplative approaches**, each receiving its own major chapter/section:

### Chapter 1: The Gradual System (rim gyis 'jug pa'i lugs)
- **Tradition**: Indian Buddhist gradualism (Kamalaśīla's approach)
- **Content**: Śamatha/vipaśyanā progressive meditation, six pāramitā practice
- **实修 value**: Foundational calm-abiding and insight instructions

### Chapter 2: The Simultaneous System (cig car 'jug pa'i lugs) — Chinese Chan
- **Tradition**: Chinese Chan/Zen as transmitted to Tibet, especially Heshang Mahāyāna's method
- **Content**: Non-conceptual meditation (*mi rtog pa*), direct access to awakening without graduated stages
- **实修 value**: HIGH — Contains actual Chan meditation instructions as practiced in Tibet. Nub ranks this ABOVE the gradual system but BELOW Vajrayāna and Dzogchen. He states it is "a pure path" while warning it could be confused with Dzogchen.
- **Key insight**: Nub includes Chan not to refute it (unlike later Tibetan tradition) but as a legitimate system with specific meditation techniques

### Chapter 3: Mahāyoga System (rnal 'byor chen po'i lugs)
- **Tradition**: Tantric deity yoga — generation stage (bskyed rim) and completion stage (rdzogs rim)
- **Content**: Visualization practices, subtle body yoga, deity identification
- **实修 value**: HIGH — Detailed tantric meditation instructions including:
  - Generation stage (deity visualization)
  - Completion stage (channels, winds, drops / rtsa rlung thig le)
  - Integration of view and practice

### Chapter 4: Atiyoga / Great Perfection System (shin tu rnal 'byor / rdzogs pa chen po'i lugs)
- **Tradition**: Early Dzogchen as understood in the 10th century
- **Content**: Non-dual contemplation, spontaneous presence, nature of mind
- **实修 value**: HIGHEST — This is the earliest extended treatment of Dzogchen meditation practice:
  - Distinguishes Dzogchen from Chan (crucial for understanding what Dzogchen IS)
  - Presents Atiyoga as both a "view" (*lta ba*) and a "mode" (*tshul*) of practice
  - Stands at the transitional point where Atiyoga shifts from being a mode of deity yoga to an independent vehicle
  - Contains specific contemplative instructions distinct from the other three systems

### Overall Framework
- The four systems are ranked hierarchically: Gradual < Simultaneous < Mahāyoga < Atiyoga
- Each section includes: (1) philosophical view, (2) meditation method, (3) conduct, (4) result
- Nub draws on both Indian and Chinese sources, plus Dunhuang-era Tibetan tantric materials

## 6. Sections Most Relevant for 实修 (Contemplative Practice)

**Priority 1 — Atiyoga/Dzogchen chapter**: The earliest systematic presentation of Dzogchen meditation as a distinct practice. Contains instructions that later became the foundation of Nyingma contemplative tradition.

**Priority 2 — Chan/Simultaneous chapter**: Preserves Chinese meditation instructions as actually practiced in Tibet — invaluable because these were later suppressed or distorted in Tibetan tradition. Includes Heshang Mahāyāna's actual method.

**Priority 3 — Mahāyoga chapter**: Generation and completion stage practices as understood in the 10th century, before the later tantric systematizations. Includes subtle body yoga.

**Priority 4 — Gradual chapter**: Standard śamatha/vipaśyanā but through the lens of a 10th-century Tibetan practitioner-scholar.

## 7. Key Insights from Research

1. **Nub Sangyé Yeshé was a practitioner, not just a scholar**: He personally received transmissions in all four systems and writes from experience
2. **The text is doxographical but practice-oriented**: Unlike later doxographies that are purely classificatory, Nub includes actual meditation instructions in each section
3. **The Chan chapter is unique in Tibetan literature**: After the legendary "Samyé debate," Chan was officially rejected. Nub is one of the last Tibetan authors to present it fairly
4. **The Dzogchen chapter captures a transitional moment**: Dzogchen is becoming independent from Mahāyoga but hasn't yet become the fully separate "vehicle" of later Nyingma tradition
5. **The text is ~500 pages in Tibetan** (pp. 575–1080 in the Kama collection = ~505 pages)
