## 1. Core Argument: Chan ≠ Dzogchen (But They Converge) {#1-core-argument}

Van Schaik's central thesis, articulated most clearly in his blog post "Tibetan Chan V: Dzogchen and Chan" (2011):

> "It seems evident that if you spend a while with Chan and Dzogchen texts from the time when the influence is supposed to have taken place (the 8th/9th centuries) that there is one clear difference between the two: **they are in dialogue with two different kinds of scripture**. That is to say, **Chan is a tradition in dialogue with the sutras, while Dzogchen is in dialogue with the tantras.**"

On the similarity question:

> "People who have said that there must be some kind of influence passing from Chan to Dzogchen have come to this belief because the texts *look similar*. However they justify the argument, it is the similarity in the language used in these two meditation traditions that caught their eye. (And this is surely true of the polemics in the Tibetan tradition itself as well as modern scholars like Guiseppe Tucci.) But, as we all know, **apparent similarities can be misleading**."

His conclusion:

> "The similarities that Dzogchen texts share with Chan texts are also shared with the sutras and tantras, while the differences show that the two genres come out of quite different environments. This is not to say that no Tibetan ever held transmissions of both Chan and Dzogchen texts; in fact it is highly likely that several did. There might have been some instances of cross-pollination. I'm not trying to hermetically seal Dzogchen away from Chan, but I hope I've shown why **arguments based on the fact that they look similar are not going to take us very far**."

Van Schaik frames his approach as showing that "when Chan and Dzogchen did come together, it was **a case of them converging at the same point, rather than one influencing the other**."

### The Shared Scriptural Basis

Van Schaik demonstrates this with a Chan text from IOL Tib J 709 attributed to Haklenayaśas (the 23rd patriarch of Chan), "The Instantaneous Approach to the Madhyamaka":

> "There are many gates to meditation in the Mahāyāna. The ultimate among them is the instantaneous approach to the Madhyamaka. The instantaneous approach has no method. One cultivates the nature of reality in this way: phenomena are mind, and mind is uncreated. In that it is uncreated, it is emptiness. Since it is like the sky, it is not a field of activity for the six sense-faculties. This emptiness is what we call vivid awareness. Yet within that vivid awareness there is no such thing as vivid awareness. Therefore without remaining in the insights gained from studying, cultivate the essential sameness of all phenomena."

Compare with the Dzogchen text from IOL Tib J 647:

> "The mind itself, which is without basis or root,  
> Is not to be found through effort; it is like the sky.  
> Enlightenment which is uncreated  
> Is enlightenment free from cause and effect."

Van Schaik notes: "I think you can probably see why there might be a temptation to say, 'they're so similar, one must have come from the other!' After all, they both talk about the mind being like the sky, and about not engaging in conventional methods to realize enlightenment. But hang on a minute — **all of this language is also found in Buddhist scripture, in both the sutras and the tantras**."

### The Key Terminological Difference: *tshor*

> "Looking back to the teaching attributed to Haklenayaśas, we see the key term 'vivid awareness' plays a key role. Now the Tibetan term behind this is *tshor*, and it is translating the Chinese character *jue* 覺. In Chan, this term means the clear and present awareness that arises in meditation."

> "This meaning of *tshor* is specific to literature translated from Chinese, and original Tibetan Chan texts based on that literature. It's a fairly central concept for Tibetan Chan texts, but elsewhere in Tibetan Buddhist writing, *tshor* means something completely different, Sanskrit *vedanā*, which is one of the five aggregates, usually translated as 'feeling'. And **tshor meaning 'vivid awareness' does not, as far as I know, ever appear in Dzogchen texts**."

## 2. bSam gtan mig sgron (Lamp for the Eyes of Contemplation) {#2-bsam-gtan-mig-sgron}

### Overview

The *bSam gtan mig sgron* (禅定目炬, "Lamp for the Eyes of Contemplation") was written by Nubchen Sangye Yeshe (gNubs Sangs rgyas ye shes) at the beginning of the 10th century. It is a major doxographical treatise that ranks different meditation traditions hierarchically.

**Full Tibetan title**: *Sgom gyi gnad gsal bar phye ba bsam gtan mig sgron*
**Found in**: *Rnying ma bka' ma shin tu rgyas pa* (v.104, pp.575-1080)

### Nubchen's Hierarchical Framework

The text ranks four approaches to meditation in ascending order:
1. **Gradualist approach** (*rim gyis 'jug pa*) — Indian scholastic Buddhism
2. **Simultaneist/Chan approach** (*cig car 'jug pa*) — Chinese Chan Buddhism
3. **Vajrayāna/Mahāyoga**
4. **Great Perfection/Atiyoga** (*rdzogs chen*)

Van Schaik notes that in this text, "**the simultaneous path is ranked above the gradualist path, but below the Vajrayāna and the Great Perfection**."

### Nubchen's Purpose for Including Chan

According to Katog Tsewang Norbu (who cites the *bSam gtan mig sgron*), Nubchen made two key statements:
1. "It is important to write about the simultaneous path **because of its similarities with the Great Perfection, which could cause a mistaken identification of the two**."
2. "**The path of Hashang Mahāyāna is a pure path**" (*yang dag pa'i lam*).

Tibetan source (from *Sa bon tsam smos pa*, p.434):
> *dgos pa ni rdzogs chen dang cha 'dra bas mi nor ba'i tshad du yin par gsung ba dang/ Hwashang gi chos de yang yang dag pa'i lam du bzhed pa'o*

### The bSam gtan mig sgron as Source for Moheyan's Teachings

The *bSam gtan mig sgron* preserves key passages of Moheyan's teachings. Van Schaik uses it alongside the Dunhuang manuscripts as a primary source.

From *bSam gtan mig sgron* p.165, Moheyan on not suppressing thoughts:
> *de bas na 'du shes dgag par yang mi bya / 'byung bzhin ci la yang mi bcos par gyi na ye gtang ji bzhin du bzhag dang rang zhi ste rjes su mi 'brang ngo*

Translation: "Therefore you should not suppress concepts. Whenever they arise, if you do not fabricate anything but instead let them go, then they will stay as they are and come to rest by themselves; thus you will not pursue them."

### Nubchen on the Debate and its Aftermath

Van Schaik notes: "the author of the 10th century *Lamp for the Eyes of Meditation*, which is all about how to rank the simultaneous and gradual methods, **fails to mention any debate**." This is significant evidence that the Samyé Debate may not have occurred as traditionally described.

### The bSam gtan mig sgron and the Evolution of Atiyoga

Van Schaik (2011, "Early Dzogchen IV"):

> "It is in the *Lamp for the Eyes of Contemplation*, which Nub wrote at the beginning of the 10th century, that he sometimes refers to Atiyoga as a vehicle. But he does so rather haphazardly. In his final summary of the differences between Mahāyoga and Atiyoga, he doesn't call them vehicles (though he doesn't call them modes either). In general **the *Lamp for the Eyes of Contemplation* stands midway between the understanding of Mahāyoga, Anuyoga and Atiyoga as *modes* of esoteric yoga, and the understanding of them as independent *vehicles*.**"

This is important because it shows the *bSam gtan mig sgron* as a transitional text in the development of the nine-vehicle system.

## 3. Moheyan's Actual Teachings from Dunhuang {#3-moheyans-actual-teachings}

### The Caricature vs. the Reality

Van Schaik consistently argues that the historical Moheyan was more sophisticated than the "cartoonish version" found in later Tibetan polemics:

> "Heshang Moheyan, the Chinese teacher of Chan...became for Tibetans a lesson in how to go wrong in meditation. He taught, they said, a method of suppressing thoughts leading to a blank state of mind that could be mistaken for enlightenment, but was really just a dead end."

But from the Dunhuang evidence (IOL Tib J 468):

> "When you are engaged in contemplation itself, look at your own mind. Then, the lack of any mental activity at all is non-thought. If there is movement of the conceptual mind, be aware of it. 'How should one be aware?' **Do not analyse the mind which is moving in terms of any kind of quality at all**: do not analyse it as moving or not moving; do not analyse it as existing or not existing; do not analyse it as virtuous or non-virtuous; and do not analyse it as defiled or pure. If you are aware of mind in this way, it is natureless. This is the practice of the dharma path."

Van Schaik comments: "He says quite clearly... he didn't advocate the suppression of thoughts."

### On the Six Perfections

Moheyan's nuanced position (from Pelliot tibétain 823):

> "According to conventional truth, the six perfections are said to be the means for teaching the ultimate truth; it is not that they are unnecessary. According to the scriptures that speak of the ultimate truth beyond the ordinary mind, there is no knowing or saying whether the other dharma methods like the six perfections are necessary. This is explained more extensively in the sutras."

Van Schaik: "Isn't this the move that the Perfection of Wisdom sutras make over and over again?"

### Self-Liberation of Thoughts

This is a key concept shared between Chan and Dzogchen. From the *bSam gtan mig sgron* (p.165), Moheyan:

> "Whenever [concepts] arise, if you do not fabricate anything but instead let them go, then they will stay as they are and **come to rest by themselves** (*rang zhi*); thus you will not pursue them."

Compare Moheyan's fifth technique (below) — "thoughts are freed the instant they arise" — with the Dzogchen concept of self-liberation (*rang grol*).

## 4. Moheyan's Five Meditation Techniques {#4-five-techniques}

From Pelliot tibétain 117 and the *bSam gtan mig sgron* (p.165), van Schaik presents Moheyan's graduated series of five techniques:

> 1. A direct awareness of the arising of deluded thoughts.
> 2. An examination of that awareness.
> 3. The prevention of the arising of thoughts.
> 4. The perception that thoughts have no intrinsic nature (that is, they are empty).
> 5. **Awareness of the arising of deluded thoughts without analysing or pursuing this awareness, so that thoughts are freed the instant they arise.**

Van Schaik notes: "It would clearly be an oversimplification to characterize Moheyan as teaching a single method for every student. It seems here that he is well aware of the need for different methods depending on the ability of the student."

The fifth technique — awareness without analysis, where thoughts self-liberate — is the one reserved for practitioners of "the sharpest faculties." For everyone else, the graduated methods apply.

**Note on "no-thought" (無念)**: The concept appears in Moheyan's teaching as *mi bsam* or *mi rtog pa* in Tibetan, but van Schaik argues it was not the blank suppression of thought that later polemicists described. Rather, it was awareness without conceptual elaboration.

## 5. The Simultaneist vs. Gradualist Framework {#5-simultaneist-gradualist}

### Key Terms

| Tibetan | Chinese | English |
|---------|---------|---------|
| *cig car gyi 'jug pa* | 頓門 (*tun-men*) | Simultaneous/sudden approach |
| *rim gyis 'jug pa* | 漸門 (*chien-men*) | Gradual approach |
| *mi rtog pa* / *mi bsam pa* | 無念 (*wu-nian*) | Non-conceptualization / no-thought |
| *bsam gtan gyi mkhan po* | 禅師 | Chan master |

### Van Schaik's Key Insight

> "Perhaps the disagreement between the two sides in the Samyé debate really comes down to this question: **who are these people with sharp faculties who can access ultimate truth directly through their own awareness?** Because for everybody else, Moheyan's teachings are not that different from the Indian and Tibetan masters he is supposed to have faced in the debate."

## 6. Chan↔Dzogchen: Similarities and Differences {#6-chan-dzogchen-comparison}

### Similarities (from shared scriptural basis)
- Mind as sky/space (*nam mkha'*)
- Non-conceptual awareness
- Beyond cause and effect for those of sharpest faculties
- Self-arising/uncreated nature of mind
- Sameness of all phenomena

### Differences (from distinct traditions)
- **Scriptural dialogue**: Chan ↔ sūtras; Dzogchen ↔ tantras
- **Key terminology**: Chan uses *tshor* (覺 *jue*) = "vivid awareness"; Dzogchen uses *rig pa* = "awareness" (Sanskrit *vidyā*)
- **Institutional context**: Chan derives from Chinese Buddhist lineages (Bodhidharma tradition); Dzogchen derives from Indian tantric lineages (attributed to Garab Dorje, Śrī Siṃha, etc.)
- **The *sems/rig pa* distinction**: Dzogchen's Instruction Series (*man ngag sde*) distinguishes between samsaric mind (*sems*) and nirvanic awareness (*rig pa*). Jigmé Lingpa argues that if one tries to stop thought without this distinction, the result is "blank indeterminacy" (*lung ma bstan*), whereas in *rig pa*, conceptualization is neutralized in a state "like a crystal ball."

### The Convergence Model

Van Schaik proposes convergence rather than influence: both traditions arrived at similar formulations because they drew on the same underlying scriptural heritage (Prajñāpāramitā, etc.), not because one derived from the other.

From "Where Chan and Tantra Meet" (van Schaik & Dalton, 2004): There is evidence that some practitioners in Sino-Tibetan areas like Dunhuang "actually combined the techniques of tantric Mahāyoga and Chan."

## 7. Nyingma Defenses of Hashang Mahāyāna {#7-nyingma-defenses}

*From van Schaik's article "The Great Perfection and the Chinese Monk" (Buddhist Studies Review 20.2, 2003; updated on earlytibet.com)*

### The Polemical Context

Khedrupjé (1385–1438), a Gelug critic:

> "Many who hold themselves to be meditators of the Snow mountains [of Tibet] talk, in exalted cryptic terms, of theory free from all affirmation, of meditative realisation free from all mentation, of [philosophical] practice free from all denial and assertion and of a fruit free from all wishes and qualms... By so doing one has proclaimed great nihilism... as well as **the thesis of the Hashang in which nothing can be the object of mentation**."

### Longchenpa's Defense (14th century)

Using the same black-and-white cloud metaphor attributed to Moheyan:

> "When the great master Hashang said this, **those of lesser intellects could not comprehend it, but it was actually the truth**."

And on the limits of simultaneous realization:

> "The great yogins who arrived at that state [of enlightenment], like Padmasambhava, Vimalamitra and Telopa, taught directly, without cause and effect, virtue or sin. Even if we understand this intellectually **we have not reached it through becoming truly accustomed to it**, so we are taught it after we have distinguished the subtle aspects of cause and effect and are no longer afraid of that state."

### Jigmé Lingpa's Defense (18th century)

From *Kun mkhyen zhal lung*:

> "You have made the assertion that the view of Hashang was like this, based on refutations such as the similarity of non-mentation to an egg. Yet scriptures such as the *Buddhāvataṃsaka* were known to Hashang. During the debate, Kamalaśīla asked what was the cause of *saṃsāra* by the symbolic action of whirling his staff around his head. [Hashang] answered that it was the apprehender and apprehended by the symbolic action of shaking his robe out twice. **It is undeniable that such a teacher was of the sharpest faculties.** If the non-recollection and non-mentation entail the offense of rejecting the wisdom of differentiating analysis, then the *Prajñāpāramitā sūtras* of the Conqueror also entail this fault. Therefore, **what the view of Hashang actually was can be known by a perfect buddha, and no one else.**"

### Katog Tsewang Norbu (18th century)

On the distinction between Chan and Dzogchen:

> "Even today in China there are Hashangs of the Chan school who teach only in the *tshung men* style. Here in Tibet too, there are a declining few who say: 'Both monks and laypeople should receive the instructions on the mind.' However, **apart from the fact that they aim for the deep inner meaning from the beginning, they have only a passing similarity to each other**."

His main point: "The Chinese teachers abandon the stages of hearing and contemplating (*thos bsam*) and make meditation (*bsam gtan*) the entire path, while **the Great Perfection contains all three stages**."

### Van Schaik's Analysis of the Defenses

> "Neither Jigmé Lingpa nor Tsewang Norbu make any attempt to identify Great Perfection with the simultaneous path. In fact both writers are careful to distance the approach of the Great Perfection of their time from the eighth-century simultaneism of Hashang Mahāyāna."

Two strategies:
1. **Longchenpa/Jigmé Lingpa**: Treat Hashang as an individual of the sharpest faculties, comparable to Padmasambhava — but place the simultaneous method beyond reach of contemporary practitioners.
2. **Nubchen/Tsewang Norbu**: Rank Chan as a valid but inferior path in a doxographic hierarchy, below Vajrayāna and Great Perfection.

## 8. Nubchen Sangye Yeshe's Treatment of Chan {#8-nubchen}

### Historical Context

Nubchen wrote at the beginning of the 10th century, during Tibet's "dark age" after the collapse of the empire. He is described as a scholar of remarkable range.

Opening of the *bSam gtan mig sgron* (Tibetan):
> *rgyu'i theg pa chen po'i lugs kyis kyang sngon ston pas zhabs kyis bcags pa'i rdo rje'i gdan dang/ bya rgod phung po'i ri dang/ li yul lcang ra smug po la stsogs pa bkra shis pa'i gnas dag bya ba grub par byed pas btsal lo*

Translation: "The way of the Mahāyāna has been sought by the accomplished in the auspicious places where our Teacher placed his feet, such as the Vajra Seat, the Vulture's Peak and the Shady Willow Grove of Khotan."

### Nubchen's Four-System Doxography

The *bSam gtan mig sgron* treats four systems:
1. **Gradualist** (*rim gyis pa*) — Chapter on the Indian gradual approach
2. **Simultaneist/Chan** (*cig car ba*) — **Chapter 2**: the Chan approach, including Moheyan's teachings
3. **Mahāyoga** — Tantric visualization and completion practices
4. **Atiyoga/Great Perfection** — The highest system

### Key Features of Chapter 2 (the Chan / *cig car* chapter)

Chapter 2 is the primary Tibetan doxographical treatment of Chinese Chan meditation. It preserves:
- Moheyan's five techniques (see §4 above)
- The teaching on non-suppression of thoughts
- Various Chan lineage teachings
- The ranking of the simultaneist approach as valid but limited

### Nubchen's Own Position

Nubchen accepted Chan as a legitimate (*yang dag pa'i lam*) but partial path. His purpose in documenting it was explicitly comparative — to prevent confusion between Chan and the Great Perfection while acknowledging their surface similarities.

From van Schaik's "Early Dzogchen IV": In Nubchen's earlier work *Armour Against Darkness* (late 9th c.), Mahā, Anu, and Ati are treated as "systems (*lugs*) representing modes (*tshul*) of practice, and not as vehicles. In fact they are specifically characterized as the lower, middle and higher divisions of a single vehicle."

## 9. Key Dunhuang Texts and Sources {#9-dunhuang-texts}

### Chan Manuscripts
- **IOL Tib J 709**: Collection of nine Chan texts, including teachings of Moheyan, the teaching of Haklenayaśas (23rd patriarch), and *The Chan Document* (authorized by Emperor Trisong Detsen's neck seal)
- **IOL Tib J 468**: Moheyan's meditation instructions (non-suppression of thoughts)
- **Pelliot tibétain 117**: Moheyan's five techniques
- **Pelliot tibétain 823**: Moheyan on the six perfections
- **Pelliot tibétain 116**: Large concertina Chan manuscript
- **Pelliot tibétain 996**: Tibetan Chan lineage text (Dro clan connection)
- **Pelliot chinois 4646**: Chinese account of the debate (emperor approves Chan)

### Dzogchen Manuscripts
- **IOL Tib J 594**: *The Small Hidden Grain* (*sBas pa'i rgum chung*) — attributed to Buddhagupta
- **IOL Tib J 647**: *The Cuckoo of Awareness* (*Rig pa'i khu byug*) — the "Six Vajra Lines"
- **IOL Tib J 470**: *Questions and Answers on Vajrasattva* — Atiyoga as a "view" within deity yoga

### Chan-Tantra Intersection
Van Schaik and Dalton (2004) argue that some Dunhuang manuscripts show "practitioners in Sino-Tibetan areas like Dunhuang actually combined the techniques of tantric Mahāyoga and Chan." The *Chan Document* in IOL Tib J 709 calls its teachings "the great yoga" (Mahāyoga).

## 10. Dylan Esler's Work {#10-esler}

### Key Publication

**Dylan Esler, *The Lamp for the Eye of Contemplation: The bSam gtan mig sgron by Gnubs chen Sangs rgyas ye shes* (Oxford University Press, 2022)**

This is the first complete English translation and critical study of the *bSam gtan mig sgron*. While the full text is behind a paywall, the following is known about Esler's key arguments:

- Esler provides a complete annotated translation of all four chapters (gradualist, simultaneist, Mahāyoga, Atiyoga)
- He analyzes Nubchen's doxographical method and its relationship to other Tibetan classification systems
- The work situates the text in its 10th-century historical context during the "dark age" of Tibetan Buddhism
- Esler examines how Nubchen uses Chan teachings both to engage with and to distinguish from Dzogchen
- The translation makes the Chan chapter (ch. 2) fully accessible for the first time in English

### Esler's Earlier Work

Esler published several articles leading up to the OUP monograph:
- Articles on Nubchen's philosophical method
- Analysis of the four systems as presented in the *bSam gtan mig sgron*
- Studies of the text's relationship to Dunhuang manuscript traditions

## Key Takeaways

1. **Van Schaik rejects the "Chan → Dzogchen influence" thesis** not by denying similarities, but by showing they derive from a shared scriptural basis (sūtras and tantras), not from direct influence.

2. **The *bSam gtan mig sgron* is the earliest systematic Tibetan treatment** of the Chan/Dzogchen relationship, ranking Chan as valid but inferior to both Vajrayāna and Great Perfection.

3. **Moheyan's actual teachings were far more nuanced** than the later caricature — he taught five graduated techniques, did not advocate blank suppression of thought, and acknowledged the conventional value of the six perfections.

4. **The self-liberation of thoughts** is a concept found in both traditions but articulated differently: Moheyan's *rang zhi* ("come to rest by themselves") vs. Dzogchen's *rang grol* ("self-liberated").

5. **Nyingma scholars defended Hashang** not by identifying Dzogchen with Chan, but by (a) treating him as an individual of exceptional faculties or (b) giving Chan a legitimate but subordinate position in a doxographic hierarchy.

6. **The *sems/rig pa* distinction** in Dzogchen's Instruction Series is a fundamental doctrinal difference from Chan — Dzogchen distinguishes between ordinary mind and primordial awareness in a way that Chan does not.

7. **Dunhuang manuscript evidence** shows Chan continued in Tibet well after the supposed "ban" following the Samyé Debate, and some practitioners combined Chan and tantric Mahāyoga methods.
