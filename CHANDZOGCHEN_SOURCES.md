## 3. Relevant Free Content Found on earlytibet.com

### 3a. "Tibetan Chan V: Dzogchen and Chan" (Nov 22, 2011)
**URL:** https://earlytibet.com/2011/11/22/tibetan-chan-v/

**Key content — directly addresses the Chan-Dzogchen question:**

Van Schaik argues that "was Dzogchen influenced by Chan" is a **bad question**. His thesis: when Chan and Dzogchen converged, it was **convergence at the same point**, not one influencing the other. Key points:

- **Shared scriptural basis, different dialogue partners:** Chan is in dialogue with the *sutras*; Dzogchen is in dialogue with the *tantras*. The similarities people notice (mind-as-sky, non-conceptuality) come from shared Buddhist scripture, not direct borrowing.

- **Technical terminology differs:** The key Chan term *tshor* (translating Chinese *jue* 覺 = vivid awareness) **never appears in Dzogchen texts**. In Dzogchen/standard Tibetan, *tshor* means *vedanā* (feeling), a completely different concept.

- **Example text analyzed:** IOL Tib J 709, teaching attributed to 'Gal na yas (= Haklenayaśas, 23rd Chan patriarch). The teaching on "instantaneous approach to the Madhyamaka" sounds superficially like Dzogchen but uses Chan-specific vocabulary.

- **Concession:** "This is not to say that no Tibetan ever held transmissions of both Chan and Dzogchen texts; in fact it is highly likely that several did."

### 3b. "The Great Perfection and the Chinese Monk: Nyingmapa Defenses of Hashang Mahāyāna"
**URL:** https://earlytibet.com/about/hashang-mahayana/

Full article (updated from *Buddhist Studies Review* 20.2, 2003: 189-204). Examines how 18th-century Nyingma scholars (Katog Tsewang Norbu, Jigmé Lingpa) defended or rehabilitated Hashang Moheyan's simultaneist approach, and its relationship to rDzogs chen.

### 3c. "Early Dzogchen I" (Jan 8, 2008)
**URL:** https://earlytibet.com/2008/01/08/early-dzogchen-i/

Discusses the two earliest known Dzogchen manuscripts from Dunhuang:
- IOL Tib J 594 ("The Small Hidden Grain") — attributed to Buddhagupta, categorized as Atiyoga
- IOL Tib J 647 ("The Cuckoo of Awareness" / "Six Vajra Lines")

Key finding: Paleographic analysis (Dalton, Davis & van Schaik 2007) shows these manuscripts date to **10th century**, not the Tibetan imperial period. They prove little about Dzogchen "authenticity" but are valuable for understanding how Dzogchen functioned in practice.

### 3d. "Early Dzogchen II" (Jan 15, 2008)
**URL:** https://earlytibet.com/2008/01/15/early-dzogchen-ii/

**Critical for understanding Chan-Tantra-Dzogchen overlap.** Discusses how Dzogchen/Atiyoga functioned as a **view applied to Mahāyoga deity yoga practice**:

- **Nyen Palyang's *Questions and Answers on Vajrasattva*** (IOL Tib J 470, early 9th c.): Dzogchen is presented as "a way (*tshul*) of practicing Mahāyoga." Atiyoga is described as a note explaining the view within deity yoga practice.

- **IOL Tib J 454:** Long tantric treatise identifying the deity with *rig pa* (awareness) of one's own enlightened mind.

- **Pelliot tibétain 44:** Padmasambhava taught "the secret *bodhicitta* included within Atiyoga" alongside Mahāyoga Vajrakīlaya sādhanas.

- **Conclusion:** "At the time of these manuscripts (9th to 10th centuries) Dzogchen/Atiyoga was primarily a view applied to the practice of deity yoga."

## 5. Summary: What We Know About PT 699 and Chan-Dzogchen Scribal Overlap

The van Schaik & Dalton 2004 paper specifically discusses **PT 699** (a Pelliot tibétain manuscript) as a commentary applying **Mahāyoga tantric structure** to a **Chan text ascribed to Bodhidharma**. This is the kind of "meeting point" the paper's title refers to — actual manuscripts where Buddhist practitioners at Dunhuang combined Chan and tantric frameworks.

The 2007 paleographic study (Dalton, Davis & van Schaik) demonstrated that **the same scribes** copied both Chan and rDzogs chen texts, proving these traditions were not isolated from each other at Dunhuang.

Van Schaik's blog posts elaborate the thesis: the convergence was not Chan→Dzogchen influence but rather **independent traditions converging** on similar non-conceptual language because both drew on the same Buddhist scriptural heritage, while maintaining distinct technical vocabularies and institutional frameworks.
